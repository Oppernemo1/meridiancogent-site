TSA overruns almost never arrive as a single, obvious failure. They arrive as a stream of small, individually-defensible decisions — one more month on IT, a slightly delayed payroll cutover, a "we'll sort the last two services out next quarter" — each of which looks minor in isolation. The cost only becomes visible in aggregate, usually well after the point where it could have been prevented cheaply.

## Extension pricing is not neutral — it's exponential

TSA pricing structures are typically set once, at signing, based on the assumption that the relationship is short-term by design. When a workstream runs past its original term, extensions rarely stay at the original rate.

**Real pricing data from mid-market carve-outs (€250–500M):**

| Service | Base Monthly | Extension Premium | Month 13–15 Cost/Month | Month 16–18 Cost/Month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ERP Support | €35k | 15% | €40.2k | €46.2k |
| IT Infrastructure | €48k | 18% | €56.6k | €66.8k |
| Finance/Accounting | €22k | 20% | €26.4k | €31.7k |
| Payroll/HR | €18k | 15% | €20.7k | €23.8k |
| Help Desk | €12k | 15% | €13.8k | €15.9k |
| **Monthly Total** | **€135k** | — | **€157.7k** | **€184.4k** |

**What this means:** A 3-month TSA extension costs not €405k (€135k × 3), it costs roughly €523k due to escalation — a 29% hidden cost on top of the base extension.

A 6-month extension costs €1.08M instead of €810k. That's a $270k premium just for being late.

## The structural reason TSA terms are always too short

Industry analysis of why TSAs consistently overrun points to a specific, repeatable failure pattern. The most common root cause: the TSA term was set against the legal closing calendar — what both sides needed the number to be in order to sign — rather than against the actual separation plan's realistic timeline.

As one integration director at a PE-backed tech carve-out described it: "We knew at signing that 12 months wasn't enough. The IT team's plan said 16–18. But the deal needed a number, the seller wanted it shorter to reduce their liability, and the buyer wanted it shorter to look aggressive in the board presentation. So we signed 12. We all knew we were leaving months of work for the TSA window."

That buy-side carve-out paid extensions for 4 additional months. At an average €160k/month, that was €640k that could have been built into the original deal price and negotiated away pre-signing.

## Carve-out complexity compounds the exposure

Separation cost is also frequently underestimated at the outset. McKinsey's analysis of carve-out cost structures has found that standalone costs for services previously delivered at parent-company scale can run as much as 200 percent higher once replicated independently.

**Example: Finance function separation**

A $300M PE acquisition of a manufacturing business. The parent company's finance function (accounting, FP&A, tax compliance) was budgeted at €180k/year across the entire company. Allocated to the divested business: €24k/year.

Actual standalone cost to replicate the function (separate entity accounting, separate tax filings, standalone FP&A, vendor management, audit coordination): €72k/year. 3x the allocation.

The buyer didn't discover this until month 7 of the TSA, when they tried to build standalone. By then, they had 5 months left on the 12-month agreement. The finance team said "we can do the transition in 5 months, but it's going to be rushed and you'll need extensions on accounting services while we sort the subsidiary structure." Result: 3 additional months of TSA extensions at elevated pricing, plus accelerated build costs to compress a 7-month project into 2 months.

Total cost overrun: €180k in TSA extensions + €120k in accelerated consulting. All because the standalone cost model wasn't built pre-signing.

## The value destruction math

Value impact at the fund level is measurable. Bain's longitudinal data on carve-out outcomes since 2012 shows an average MOIC of roughly 1.5x, with top-quartile execution delivering closer to 2.5x. That gap between average and top-quartile execution is, in large part, a story about how well the TSA period was managed.

**Here's the calculation:**

For a $400M enterprise value acquisition with a 2.0x MOIC target:
- Entry: $400M
- Exit value target: $800M
- Implied value creation: $400M

If TSA overruns, discovery costs, and extended transition services eat $25M (realistic for a complex carve-out), that's:
- New exit value: $775M
- New MOIC: 1.94x
- Value destruction: 6 basis points

In fund return terms, that's material. A 1.5x deal that should have been 2.0x doesn't make the LP target.

## Why the fix usually isn't "negotiate a longer TSA"

The instinctive response to overrun risk is to negotiate more time upfront. In practice, this often just delays the same problem: a longer TSA still lacks the defined exit criteria, service-level ownership, and acceptance testing that actually determine whether a service is transitioned or merely "still running."

What actually works: carve-out programmes that apply discipline to separation planning report dramatically compressed TSA timelines. Advisory analysis has documented exits completed in as little as 2 to 3 months post-close where the separation plan, rather than the legal calendar, drove the timeline from day one.

The distinguishing factor isn't TSA duration. It's whether every service inside the TSA has:
- A named owner (buyer side and seller side)
- A defined completion standard (not "we'll know it when we see it")
- A tracked exit date (with real consequences for missing it)
- A live view of where each workstream actually stands

That visibility costs almost nothing to build. The lack of it costs millions.

## Lessons learned

**From working with integration teams:**

- **Extensions are not plan failures, they're pricing failures.** You didn't fail to exit the service — you just failed to price for the reality. Build a 20–30% contingency into the TSA cost model and call it what it is.

- **Month 13 is exponentially more expensive than month 12.** Don't defer difficult decisions into the extension window. A hard call in month 11 (cut over with temporary workarounds, or extend now and lock in better pricing) is cheaper than discovering you need to extend in month 13.

- **The buyer's runway matters more than the seller's mood.** If the buyer's team says they need 16 months, believe them. You can't negotiate a separation into 12 months. You can only negotiate the price for the reality.

- **Stranded costs are invisible until the seller's P&L is separate.** That $10M IT budget? The seller is keeping it even after you leave, because the infrastructure doesn't shrink. Model for that explicitly in the deal economics.

- **Service escalation should be aggressive, by design.** 15–25% uplift per month on extensions isn't punishment — it's the only mechanism that makes a temporary cost stay temporary. Without it, the service runs forever at a slightly higher price.
