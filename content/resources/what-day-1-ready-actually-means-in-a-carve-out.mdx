"Day 1 ready" is one of the most confidently used phrases in a carve-out — and one of the least consistently defined. Different functions in the same deal often mean different things by it: IT means systems are accessible, finance means books can technically be produced, HR means people are paid. None of those, individually or together, actually confirm the new entity can operate as a standalone business the moment the deal closes.

## A stricter, more useful definition

A tighter working definition: **Day 1 readiness means the divested entity can transact, close its books, and serve customers using only its own systems and data, from the legal close date forward** — not a partial state, not a best-effort approximation, and not something patched together with informal workarounds against the parent's systems.

A practical acceptance test that cuts through the ambiguity: can the new entity produce a clean trial balance, generate an accurate customer invoice, and fulfill an order end-to-end, using only its own systems? If any of those three fail in a pre-close rehearsal, the entity is not Day 1 ready — regardless of how complete the infrastructure looks on a status slide.

## The readiness framework that actually works

Rather than a vague "ready / not ready" status, use this maturity model:

| Dimension | Level 1: Planning | Level 2: Executing | Level 3: Testing | Level 4: Locked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Systems** | Target systems identified | Systems provisioned, config underway | Full mock cutover passed | No changes post-lock |
| **Data** | Source system mapped | Data extraction built | Full data validation done | Data frozen, locked |
| **Processes** | To-be process defined | Process guides written | Process run end-to-end | Live rehearsal passed |
| **Governance** | Roles assigned | Daily standups active | Escalation log maintained | Zero open decisions |
| **Readiness gate** | Month 3–4 | Month 6–8 | Month 10–11 | Month 11–12 (freeze) |

**Real example: A €320M industrial carve-out**

A PE buyer acquired a manufacturing business from a Fortune 500 conglomerate. The readiness plan:

- **Month 1–3 (Planning):** Systems identified (SAP for ERP, Salesforce for CRM, legacy billing system), data sources mapped, process gaps identified
- **Month 4–6 (Executing):** SAP instance provisioned, customer master data extracted, new billing system configured, org structure set up
- **Month 7–9 (Testing):** Full end-to-end trial run: customer orders placed, invoices generated, payments recorded, monthly close completed using only standalone systems
- **Month 10–11 (Locked):** Scope frozen, data locked, systems hardened, go-live plan finalized

The business was genuinely Day 1 ready because they'd run the entire operation as a standalone for 60 days in the lab. When close arrived, they flipped the switch. No surprises.

Contrast: another carve-out in the same portfolio said they were "ready" at month 8, had never run a full mock close, and discovered at go-live that their tax reporting process couldn't run standalone (it depended on parent-level consolidations they'd never extracted). They lived on a TSA for tax support for 8 additional months.

## Why the gap between "looks ready" and "is ready" is where failures hide

Carve-out complexity, especially in multi-country or entangled businesses, means readiness gaps are almost always hidden until you try to operate independently. A system looks functional. Data looks migrated. Then you try to close the books and discover that customer credit limits are tied to parent company master records. Or that product cost allocation logic lives in a formula in a spreadsheet nobody documented. Or that the tax return process assumes a consolidated entity structure.

FTI's carve-out due diligence research documents this plainly: "Standard financial and operational due diligence often misses structural entanglements such as underestimated IT separation costs, poorly defined transitional service agreements, and deferred people-related liabilities. These blind spots frequently surface post-close and erode value if not identified upfront."

What makes this worse: discovering these gaps post-close is exponentially more expensive. A process that doesn't work in the lab can be rearchitected on a timeline. A process that doesn't work post-close requires TSA extensions and firefighting while running live.

## The cost of failing Day 1 readiness

**Data readiness workstreams for a mid-market carve-out typically run:**
- Duration: 4–9 months
- Cost: €500k–3M depending on system complexity and number of applications
- Extended TSA cost (per month of delay): €100k–250k

**Real numbers:** If data readiness isn't finished by close and carries into month 3 post-close, the cost math looks like:

```
Original data readiness cost:        €1.2M (4 months, €300k/month)
Extended TSA (3 additional months):  €450k (€150k/month × 3)
Accelerated build costs (crunch):   €200k
Post-close firefighting:             €150k
---
Total actual cost:                   €2.0M
(Original estimate was €1.2M — 67% overrun)
```

And that's just direct cost. The indirect cost is higher: finance teams running duplicate processes (parent + standalone) until cutover is complete; compliance risk on financial reporting; customer-facing disruptions if billing systems don't work cleanly.

## Building readiness into the pre-close timeline

The carve-outs that hold up under scrutiny treat Day 1 readiness as a pre-close discipline, tested well before signing rather than assembled reactively afterward. The mechanism is consistent across successful deals:

**1. Run full mock cutover rehearsals before close** — two rehearsals minimum, 60 days apart. The second one should be a complete dry-run of go-live, including finance team closing the books, IT team managing cutover, HR running a pay cycle, and operations fulfilling orders.

**2. Freeze scope changes in the final weeks before close** — most Day 1 failures trace back to dependencies that were never tested end-to-end, combined with last-minute scope creep introduced right before go-live. As one integration director told us: "We kept discovering new requirements in week 11. By week 12, we'd built them in but hadn't tested them. That's how we ended up on a TSA for our own systems."

**3. Own the standalone cost model** — don't build readiness plans against allocated costs. Run a bottoms-up rebuild: what does it actually cost to run this function standalone? Often 40–60% higher than parent allocations. When you find that gap, you fix the plan or adjust the deal — before close, not after.

**4. Test the three non-negotiables:**
- **Can the business close its books?** Full P&L and balance sheet, with no parent-level entries, using only standalone systems
- **Can the business invoice customers?** Orders placed, products shipped, invoices generated and sent, payments received — all in standalone systems
- **Can the business run payroll?** Employee data, tax withholding, benefits administration, all using standalone systems

If any of those three fail in a rehearsal, you're not Day 1 ready.

## Lessons learned

**From working with integration teams:**

- **"Looks ready" and "is ready" are different states.** A system that's 85% configured is not 85% ready. It's not ready until you've run the entire business through it. Invest in mock rehearsals. The cost of a 60-day lab run is €50–100k. The cost of discovering you need it post-close is €500k+.

- **Data readiness is 60% of the work, not 20%.** Every integration plan says "data readiness is critical." Most still underestimate it. Your data separation timeline should be your critical path, not an afterthought. If your IT team says 6 months and your data team says 10, believe the data team.

- **Standalone cost ≠ allocated cost.** That €50k annual allocation for a shared function? It's €100k standalone, maybe more. If you plan against the allocation, you're planning to fail. Build the real model and cost the gap.

- **The freeze date is the discipline.** 30 days before close, all discovery stops. No new requirements, no scope changes. If you find something, you either cut it or extend the TSA. But you don't build and deploy it for the first time at go-live.

- **Day 1 readiness is not the finish line.** Bain's research notes: "Getting to Day 1 and getting real value from the carve-out are not the same milestone." Day 1 is when the business starts. Month 3 is when you know if the separation actually worked.
