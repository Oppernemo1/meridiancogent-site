Most separation leaders operate with a hidden assumption: the earlier you start planning the separation, the better positioned you are. It sounds logical. More time means more discovery, more diligence, more runway. But that assumption breaks down the moment you actually have to separate a business that is still changing shape.

A carve-out separation plan built before the deal is fully scoped is a plan built against an unknown target. The business you're separating is not the business you thought it was when you started. Key contracts are renegotiated in due diligence. Divestitures are added or removed from scope. Tax structuring changes. Systems strategy shifts. By the time you close, the plan you built months ago is obsolete in exactly the ways that matter most.

And yet, start too late — after a buyer is selected, or worse, after a deal is signed — and you're trapped. You have six months of running room to separate a business that has never operated alone, and you haven't yet discovered what entanglements actually exist.

## The window is real, and it's specific

The operational data on this is surprisingly consistent. A parent company that begins separation planning **six months before putting a business on the market** can present a credibly scoped carve-out to bidders. Not a guessed-at scope, not an estimated cost model — an actual map of what the business is, what's entangled, and what it will take to separate it.

That early work is not a separation plan in the execution sense. It's a **separation readiness assessment**. Understand what systems are shared. Identify contract assignment requirements. Map the dependency tree. Quantify the cost structure as a standalone entity, not as a parent-allocated abstraction. Flag the long-lead items — software licensing renegotiations, regulatory approvals, infrastructure buildout.

EY's carve-out research documents this clearly: "Planning performed prior to deal-signing pays dividends." Not because you can execute the entire separation before signing, but because you can scope it precisely. You know what needs to happen, who needs to own it, and what you're buying time with a TSA to achieve.

## Real timeline: When planning actually matters

Here's where separation planning creates value in a carve-out timeline:

```
Pre-Marketing Planning:        Separation readiness assessment
6 months before marketing      ↓
↓ 4–6 weeks of work, informs deal structure & pricing
↓
At Market Launch:              Deal structure locked, buyer diligence begins
3–4 months during process      ↓
↓ Buyer validates scope, confirms separation feasibility
↓
After Signing:                 Execution plan locked, separation begins
9–15 months until close        ↓
↓ Build separation workstreams, systems, processes
↓
At Close:                      Business operates standalone
Day 1                          ↓
```

**Real example: A €280M tech carve-out**

A PE buyer was acquiring a software product line from a Fortune 500 tech conglomerate.

**Pre-marketing (6 months before):** The seller ran a 4-week separation readiness assessment. They discovered:
- 47 shared systems (not the 12 they initially thought)
- 3 long-lead software license renegotiations (6–8 months each)
- A shared data center with 18 months of exit lead time
- 11 customer contracts with parent company co-obligations (renegotiation required)

That assessment informed the deal: scope was adjusted, pricing was adjusted to reflect separation cost, and the buyer knew exactly what they were buying.

**After signing (12 months pre-close):** The separation plan was executed against known, realistic scope. The 47 systems were prioritized. The license renegotiations started immediately. The data center exit plan was sequenced. No surprises.

**At close:** The business ran on standalone infrastructure. No TSA needed for core systems. A 3-month TSA for knowledge transfer only.

**Contrast:** Another tech carve-out in the same space. The seller waited to start separation planning until after a buyer was selected (month 6 into the sale process). They discovered 6 weeks later (month 7.5) that a critical ERP instance was shared, required a 12-month migration to separate, and the close date was already 9 months out. They built a 14-month TSA, missed the timeline anyway, and extended twice.

The seller's readiness assessment cost would have been €40k and 4 weeks. The missed timeline cost them €2.3M in TSA extensions and buyer renegotiations.

## Why the timing matters more than the effort

A separation readiness assessment built before marketing takes four to six weeks with the right team. It is substantially less work than building a full separation execution plan. And it is exponentially more valuable than a plan built against a moving target.

One separation office director described it: "We started planning the separation when we had 8 months to close. We thought that was plenty of time. We discovered, very quickly, that our understanding of what was entangled was wrong. We kept finding new systems and dependencies. By month 7, we were 3 weeks from close and still discovering new scope. We extended the TSA by design, but we looked unprepared to the buyer."

The reason is simple: **information asymmetry compounds over time.** The later you start planning, the more of the deal structure is already set in concrete — and the less room you have to ask whether the separation is actually feasible within the buyer's timeline and budget.

A readiness assessment done pre-marketing can influence deal structuring and pricing. The same assessment done post-signing can only document the constraints it discovered.

## The practical sweet spot for starting

The ideal moment to launch a separation readiness assessment is: **When the board has authorized a potential divestiture but before the business goes to market.**

That gives you a window of four to eight weeks to:
- Map the real separation cost
- Flag the regulatory or contractual blockers
- Identify the long-lead items
- Feed that information into deal structuring, vendor negotiations, and buyer marketing

A seller who does this work finds that it either accelerates the close (because the scope is clear and undisputed) or surfaces a problem early enough to fix it (because you found it before a buyer had to).

A seller who defers it discovers at close why the separation was harder than anyone expected — which is when the TSA term suddenly becomes the most expensive provision in the entire agreement.

## Lessons learned

**From working with separation teams:**

- **Six months pre-marketing is the real deadline.** If you're thinking about a divestiture, start the readiness assessment as soon as the decision is made, not after. That 4-week assessment costs €40–60k and informs pricing. Skipping it costs millions later.

- **The readiness assessment is not the separation plan.** People often confuse the two. The assessment is "what's entangled and what will it cost?" The plan is "how and when do we separate it?" You need both, but they're different documents for different audiences.

- **Deal scope changes kill separation plans.** If the scope changes post-signing (entities added or removed, contracts renegotiated, tax structure revised), your separation plan is partially obsolete. Build contingency. Treat the 4-week mark post-signing as a "plan refreeze" moment where you validate against the new deal structure.

- **The buyer's separation knowledge is critical post-signing.** Buyers often have a view on systems, processes, and dependencies from their diligence. Incorporate that immediately. It either validates your plan or surfaces gaps you missed.

- **Long-lead items own your timeline.** A 12-month software license renegotiation or a 18-month data center exit cannot be compressed. If those are on the critical path, the TSA term is locked. You can't negotiate your way out of physics.
